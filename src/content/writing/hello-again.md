So it has been a long time since I updated my website. Well, it has been a journey: six years. Recapping it will take some time, but I have a few paragraphs to share what I learned from various stints and the things I am grateful for. I will leave what's next for me for the next article. What is certain, though, is that an academic path feels like the only path that allows for the intellectual freedom I need at this stage. It allows me to aim high and plant seeds that might bear fruit in the future rather than pruning hedges ([Price et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-025-01974-7)).

Wadhwani AI was such a roller-coaster ride. I spent nearly two years working there, focusing on multiple projects. I primarily worked on two projects. The [first project](https://www.wadhwaniai.org/impact/healthcare-solutions/line-probe-assay/) focused on automating interpretation of a Tuberculosis test caled Line Probe Assay (LPA). I was part of a three-person team. I had so much fun working on it: high ownership and clearly scoped work made it much easier to make the project a success. During this project, I am glad to have met [Keshav](https://www.linkedin.com/in/keshav-ramaswami/) and [Dr. Rahul](https://www.linkedin.com/in/rahulap/). I couldn't have asked for a better first project at the start of my career. Since it gave me the much needed confidence in my abilities with the exterenal success it came with. After my first project, I wanted to focus on agriculture, and therefore I joined the [Cotton Ace](https://www.wadhwaniai.org/impact/agriculture-solutions/crop-ace/) project. I was part of a large ML team, where I focused on improving our deployed object-detection model by adding a rejection model to abstain from making predictions on photos that were dark, blurry or just outside the intended settings, like a photo of a car. This project especifically introduced me to concept shift for our project, since we now had a new business logic where we could just reject a photo and request another photo for predictions. Given the more mature nature of the second project, it moved slow, needed approvals in order to not break existing deployments and was generally less exciting. On both projects, however, I loved that I was able to see deployments in the field. In every field deployment I have always been surprised because you learn how people use your work. And in every-single-one of these visits, I have been surprised. I am grateful to have met very kind people during my time at Wadhwani AI. I cherish the friendships I found with fellow associates, [Nikhil](https://shenoynikhil.com), [Pulkit](https://madaanpulkit.github.io), [Anmol](https://www.linkedin.com/in/anmolojha15/), [Chandan](https://www.linkedin.com/in/chan4899/), and [Makkunda](https://makkunda.github.io). [Jigar](https://www.jigarkdoshi.com) and [Jerome](https://www.linkedin.com/in/jerome-white/) mentored our team, and I find them great at breaking down complexity. Lastly, during my final few months at Wadhwani, I saw how deeply a company's direction and culture are anchored in the people who hold executive roles. When Wadhwani went through an overhaul, with new people filling major executive positions, it felt as if I were in a new organization. I certainly realized this much later.

Next came Rephrase AI. I was amazed by the concentration of brainpower at the organization. I remember that whenever I was stuck on a training problem, my manager, [Nisheeth](https://www.linkedin.com/in/nisheethlahoti/), would look at the loss curve and predict that a certain function was preventing certain parameters from being learned. This was the first time I saw how an intuitive understanding of mathematics could help you see solutions that are far from obvious otherwise. It was nothing short of impressive how the team was able to achieve seemingly impossible things by thinking from first principles and because they really wanted to solve the problem of virtual avatars. I also realized that I joined Rephrase because I wanted to be closer to SOTA research, be in Bangalore and joining a startup seemed exciting. Although, I also realized being indifferent to the idea of creating virtual avatars made it harder for me to push. It taught me an important lesson: work on things you _believe in_ and can imagine pursuing for years without becoming bored. I _know_ the power of compounding, but still the short-term excitement often should be experienced with a pinch of salt. Even after learning this, it can be hard to keep my inherent curiosity in the driver's seat consistently.

Finally, I came to Tübingen to continue my studies because I never planned to not get a master's degree, but it was COVID-19 that had changed the course of my life in 2020. It was time to return to that dream. I was driven by both curiosity about the world outside India and, to some extent, a desire to return to research. Landing in Germany and immediately signing up for 150% of the suggested credits for my first semester wasn't smart. Once again, I wasn't giving curiosity space to guide me; instead, I was forcing myself to be "productive". Maybe the need to feel "productive" is a result of a scarcity mindset? I am not sure, but it certainly makes you choose things you would not if you were okay being average. The last two and a half years have been intense. I have grown so much as a human that it feels strange not to have moved earlier. Throwing yourself into a different country allows you to see the customs of your home country for what they are: customs. Moving certainly teaches you so much about the world and yourself. Having moved to Europe has made me much more attentive about my health. In India, it was always an afterthought, I would order food on these food delivery application to put in a few extra hours. Here, just the fact that being a student pushes you to save money but also be very mindful of what you are eating. I learned so much about my own body and how different hormones work around my body, that I experimented creating a startup that helped people better understand their bodies. My idea was to help people experiment with their own bodies based on current medical scientific research. A lofty goal riddled with numerous blockades. I am personally much fitter today than I was when I moved from India, and it shows in my blood reports from [Apoorv Pathology](https://www.apoorvpathology.com) whenever I get to visit home.

While in Tübingen, I also got to experiment with working in traditionally large companies. My experience at Mercedes was a mixed bag. Life is certainly more organized when working for a large company than at a startup, but one of the problems I faced was the language barrier. My German today is at the B1–B2 level. During my time at Mercedes, when my German was perhaps at the A2 level, I remember being in group meetings held in German. I would not understand the discussion, which effectively meant that I wasn't in the meeting. Lunch conversations would always happen in German. This experience was very disappointing, but there is no quick, logical solution: if team members are simply more comfortable in German, you can't switch that easily. This gave me an insight that I still hold: I wouldn't be able to work at a place where the lingua franca is not English. This rules out large organizations in Germany and elsewhere in Europe where the local language dominates the workplace. I am still working on my German, but I can't imagine switching to German for my work, where deep understanding is so important.

Lastly, in order to hone my skills in building a startup, I approached Dr. Wieland Brendel and he offered a thesis on creating an AI powered tool for teachers for lesson plans. This work is to be part of Germany's new open-source nationalwide Adaptive Intelligent System learning platform for schools. To develop our prototype, we designed the thesis to follow a mixed-medthods study. It was split in two phases, requirements collection and evaluation of the prototype based on those requirements. During phase one, I first conducted numerous interviews and planned the primary features of the tool. Next came the development, which involved a lot of sessions with Claude Code and fixing bugs. At last, I evaluated the prototype with and without AI support in form of a between subject study design. In such a study, a participant only is allowed to take part in one condition so you need more participants to have statistical significance. One thing that I learned from this study was: it is so time consuming to conduct grounded research. And it reminded again about having this inherent drive to solve a perticular problem to sustain the persistence needed when eventually your motivation dips.

Given that I want curiosity to be in the driver's seat of the work I do, I can only see that happening in academia. I want to be able to answer questions using the scientific method; at this stage, I wish for nothing more than for some of those answers to help move the needle a few years down the line.

Until next time. :)
